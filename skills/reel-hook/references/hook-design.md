# Mechanisms, not magic words

These are creative options, not proven rankings. Choose for audience relevance and available evidence. Original examples below are hypothetical, not real product claims.

| Mechanism | Opening example / picture | Payoff and product bridge | Failure to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Result first | Show the finished desk; “The smallest change is under the cables.” | Reveal how the real organizer fits | A beautiful result unrelated to the item |
| Recognizable frustration | A drawer jams on the same utensil twice | Demonstrate the appropriate divider | Shaming the viewer |
| Diagnostic detail | “Measure this before choosing a planter.” | Show inner diameter and fit | Turning a tray into a watering system |
| Fair comparison | Two containers, one real shared constraint | Explain the tradeoff and who each suits | Fake competition or unequal conditions |
| Counterexpectation | “The larger pot isn't always the better fit.” | Explain this specific fit case | Unsupported universal contrarian claims |
| Specific information gap | One part does not match; “Why is this edge different?” | Show the actual design purpose | Vague “secret” with no answer |
| Process compression | Clay → forming → finished ceramic, using matched geometry | Real process with time compression clear | Implying instant manufacture |
| Maker decision | Two rejected prototypes beside the final design | Show what changed and why | Invented customer requests |
| Sensory event | Close-up of a real magnetic closure meeting | Hear and see the closure in normal use | Fake sound implying unavailable mechanics |
| Visual mystery | Macro texture resolves to a recognizable product | Reveal early enough to understand use | An unrecognizable opening that never earns interest |
| Impossible-to-real | A flat product silhouette is picked up as an object | Product becomes part of a small visual story | Spectacle with a random sales pitch |
| Scale play | Miniature desk resolves into full-size workspace | Establish true size before purchase CTA | Misrepresenting product dimensions |
| Match action | Hand crosses frame; same grip now holds another finish | Compare real available variants | Morphing into nonexistent options |
| Reverse construction | Pieces assemble in reverse | Reveal actual finished object; show normal use | Implying self-assembly |
| Absurdist list | “One plant. Two plants. A new furniture problem.” | Reveal actual space-management idea | False prices or a nonsensical product pivot |
| Identity / recipient | A plant lover negotiates for one more windowsill spot | Recognizable situation worth sending to a partner | “Tag three friends” with no value |
| Genuine objection | “What fits inside this one?” | Show dimensions and a real fit example | Hiding limitations |
| Useful checklist | Three visible things to check before ordering | Deliver all three clearly | Artificially withholding the last answer |
| Open experiment | “Which arrangement leaves room for the keyboard?” | Fair visible comparison with answer | Calling AI footage a real test |
| Small story | Object does not fit → change one choice → fit | Product solves the established problem | Long introduction before anything happens |
| Restrained anti-spectacle | One crisp, uninterrupted useful action | Proof is the attraction | Assuming slow is automatically premium or viral |
| Honest boundary | “Pick this version if you need X; skip it if you need Y.” | Credible buyer qualification | Invented benefits or performative negativity |

## The user's two reference mechanisms

**“$0 eggs”**: the original Reel was not supplied. Its joke, sponsorship, and explanation are unknown. Analyze as familiar format → anomalous claim → interpretation/payoff, not as verified cognitive dissonance or a proven retention winner. If a price joke could be taken as an offer, make the joke unmistakable and give the real offer. Prefer absurd behavior or scale over false price claims.

**Floating pizza/cup**: flat-looking object → physical reach → occlusion at contact → depth/weight change → bite/sip with appropriate sound. The reveal supplies the reward. For a product Reel, make the product's real use part of the consequence. A cup normally suggests a sip, not a crunchy bite. Do not infer how the original was made without footage or a breakdown.

## Select visual triggers deliberately

Anticipation, impossibility, and danger are optional mechanisms, not three mandatory ingredients or an exhaustive psychology model. Name one dominant trigger for a visual concept; add a secondary trigger only when it strengthens the same understandable event. A clear demonstration, sensory reward, or relatable joke may need none of these three.

| Trigger | Viewer question | Useful application | Review condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anticipation | What happens next? | Reveal, pending contact, experiment, unexpected response | Specify the exact unresolved event and its payoff; deliver progress before the end |
| Impossibility | How can that happen? | Surreal product choreography, scale play, physical contradiction | Make the anomaly readable; preserve product identity; distinguish fiction from real capability |
| Danger / threatened consequence | Will something go wrong? | Fictional near-miss, ordinary spill or loss, relatable problem | Stakes must fit the brand; resolve them; never treat rendered rescue as evidence of protection or durability |

For reach, test novelty and share motivation. For sales, prioritize the product's causal role, useful evidence, and the next action; more tension is not automatically better. Compare trigger variants against a straightforward product demonstration, holding the offer and main body stable where feasible. Evaluate retention alongside qualified actions, not public views alone.

When collecting actual references, preserve source URL, creator, access limits, approximate observed beats, payoff, product bridge, and performance status. Distinguish a reusable mechanism from its execution variants. Mark reconstructed prompts as adaptations, never creator originals. Reuse available project library entries by audience/product/objective fit; do not label an untested entry a winner.

## Editorial review rubric

Score each dimension 0–2, with a one-line justification. 0 = absent/conflicting, 1 = plausible but underspecified, 2 = concrete and aligned. Total /14 is an internal comparison, never a performance forecast.

1. **Relevance:** a recognizable viewer need/situation, not just a broad demographic.
2. **Clarity:** subject and action readable at phone size, including muted viewing.
3. **Tension or attraction:** a specific question, desirable result, recognizable joke, or sensory reward.
4. **Payoff:** the exact promise visibly fulfilled, with a named beat/time.
5. **Product fit/proof:** product causally belongs; claims and reference assets support it.
6. **Feasibility:** actions, speech, composition, asset fidelity, and timing can coexist.
7. **Objective fit:** appropriate share motivation or buyer action, without contradictory CTA stacking.

Truth is a gate: a deceptive 14/14 concept still fails. Low novelty may be acceptable when clear product proof serves buying intent. A funny high-reach concept may be unsuitable for immediate conversion. Report these tradeoffs.

## Beat planning

For a 15–25s concept, an initial planning hypothesis is: 0–2s recognizable event; 2–6s first reward/context; middle demonstration or escalation; payoff with enough time to understand; short next action. These windows are adjustable, not platform requirements. Name each beat's purpose and remove beats that contribute nothing. A re-hook introduces a meaningful new question after progress, not another “wait until the end.”

Loop only when it improves the experience after the promise is fulfilled. Do not interrupt the answer to force a replay. Hold captions long enough to read when allowed; do not default to single-word flashes. Give the muted viewer intelligible action even when brand rules prohibit overlaid text.
