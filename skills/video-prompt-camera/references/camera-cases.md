# Camera case names and distinctions

## Contents

- [Cases](#cases)
- [Terminology references](#terminology-references)

Adapted from Shotcraft camera atlas, reviewed 2026-11-20. Example directions are original, untested instructions. Terminology varies by crew; descriptive combinations are not claimed as standard industry nicknames. Read only the relevant case.

## Cases

### Pan

Also used: Pan left, Pan right.

Turn left or right from a fixed camera position. Pan changes the aim. Truck or track changes the camera position.

Example direction: Pan right from the maker to the finished vase; keep the camera in place.

### Tilt

Also used: Tilt up, Tilt down.

Aim up or down from a fixed camera position. 

Example direction: Tilt up from the shoes to the face; keep the camera height fixed.

### Camera roll

Rotate the camera around its viewing axis during the shot. A roll changes the cant during the shot; a Dutch angle can stay fixed.

Example direction: Roll the camera clockwise while keeping the face centered.

### Whip pan

Also used: Swish pan.

A rapid pan transfers attention across the scene. Swish pan is an alternative name. The word whip describes the rapid move, not a different axis.

Example direction: Whip pan right from the doorway to the person entering.

### Push in

Also used: Dolly in.

Move the camera toward the subject. Push in describes the travel. Specify a dolly if that is the rig you intend; a zoom is a different operation.

Example direction: Push in toward the cup, then ease to a stop on the rim.

### Pull back

Also used: Dolly out, Pull out.

Move the camera away from the subject. Pull back describes the travel away. Do not substitute a zoom-out.

Example direction: Pull back from the face to reveal the room.

### Lateral tracking shot

Also used: Truck left, Truck right.

Move the camera sideways. Tracking is broader than a sideways move. Add left, right, toward or away rather than relying on the label alone.

Example direction: Truck right past the foreground post; keep the camera aimed at the wall.

### Pedestal up / down

Raise or lower the camera while keeping its aim unchanged. 

Example direction: Pedestal up from table height; keep the lens level.

### Boom up / down

Raise or lower the camera on a supporting arm. Name the camera move and the rig separately. A jib or crane describes the equipment, not one fixed route.

Example direction: Boom up to reveal the people behind the foreground wall.

### Arc shot

Also used: Arc left, Arc right.

Travel around part of the subject while aiming toward it. Orbit is useful for circling a subject. Specify the extent of the arc rather than assuming a complete circle.

Example direction: Arc around the subject to camera right, maintaining the same distance.

### Aerial orbit

Circle a subject from an airborne platform. 

Example direction: Orbit the building from the air, keeping the roof centered.

Descriptive instruction, not a standardized nickname.

### Spiral in

Move around the subject while closing the distance. 

Example direction: Circle the sculpture while gradually closing the distance.

Descriptive instruction, not a standardized nickname.

### Arc and rise

Combine an arc around the subject with increasing height. 

Example direction: Arc around the sculpture while raising the camera.

Descriptive instruction, not a standardized nickname.

### Follow shot

Move with a travelling subject. Tracking shot is the broader term. This example specifies following from behind.

Example direction: Track behind the walker, keeping the following distance unchanged.

### Leading tracking shot

Travel backward in front of a moving subject. 

Example direction: Track backward ahead of the walker, keeping their face framed.

Descriptive instruction, not a standardized nickname.

### Side tracking shot

Travel alongside a moving subject. 

Example direction: Track alongside the walker, matching their pace.

Descriptive instruction, not a standardized nickname.

### Zoom in

Narrow the lens angle of view from the same position. Crash zoom, snap zoom and whip zoom refer to a rapid zoom, not every zoom-in.

Example direction: Zoom in on the face without moving the camera.

### Zoom out

Widen the lens angle of view from the same position. A rapid zoom-out can also be a crash or snap zoom. State both direction and speed.

Example direction: Zoom out to reveal the table while keeping the camera fixed.

### Dolly zoom

Also used: Vertigo effect.

Coordinate camera travel with an opposing zoom to hold subject size. The opposing zoom and camera travel distinguish this from an ordinary push-in.

Example direction: Dolly toward the subject while zooming out to hold their size in frame.

### Rack focus

Also used: Rack.

Shift sharp focus between subjects at different distances. Pulling focus also includes keeping a moving subject sharp. A rack specifies a shift between focus targets.

Example direction: Rack focus from the cup in the foreground to the maker behind it.

### Wide-angle lens

Choose a broad angle of view for the selected sensor or image format. 

Example direction: Use a wide-angle lens and specify the camera distance separately.

### Long lens

Also used: Telephoto.

Choose a narrow angle of view for the selected image format. 

Example direction: Use a long lens; choose the camera position to frame the subject tightly.

### Fisheye lens

Use a deliberately curved image projection. 

Example direction: Use a fisheye projection with visibly curved edges near the frame boundary.

### Eye-level shot

Place the camera near the subject’s eye height. 

Example direction: Set the lens at the seated subject's eye height.

### High-angle shot

Place the camera above the subject and look downward. 

Example direction: Place the camera above the subject and angle it downward.

### Low-angle shot

Place the camera below the subject and look upward. 

Example direction: Place the camera below the face and angle it upward.

### Ground-level shot

Place the camera close to the ground. Ground level specifies camera height. Worm’s-eye implies an upward-looking view and is not a synonym for every low camera.

Example direction: Set the camera close to the floor and look level across it.

Descriptive instruction, not a standardized nickname.

### Overhead shot

Also used: Top-down shot.

Position the camera directly above and look down. Top-down makes the aim explicit. Bird’s-eye can describe a broader elevated view.

Example direction: Frame the table from directly overhead, looking straight down.

### Dutch angle

Also used: Canted angle.

Hold the frame at a cant around its viewing axis. 

Example direction: Hold a slight Dutch angle; do not roll during the take.

### Profile shot

Also used: Side view.

View the subject from the side. 

Example direction: Frame the subject in profile, looking toward frame left.

### Rear view

View the subject from behind. 

Example direction: Frame the subject from behind as they look toward the doorway.

Descriptive instruction, not a standardized nickname.

### Three-quarter view

View the subject between frontal and profile positions. 

Example direction: Frame the face between frontal and profile, with the near eye clearly visible.

Descriptive instruction, not a standardized nickname.

### Point-of-view shot

Also used: POV.

Frame what a character is looking at. 

Example direction: Show the object from the character's eyeline as their point of view.

### Over-the-shoulder shot

Also used: OTS, OS.

Include a foreground shoulder while framing another subject. A dirty single includes part of another person in the foreground; it need not show as much shoulder as this OTS. A clean single excludes that foreground person.

Example direction: Shoot an OTS toward the listener, keeping a little of the speaker's shoulder in frame.

### Selfie shot

Face the camera back toward the person holding it. 

Example direction: Face the camera back toward the person holding it.

Descriptive instruction, not a standardized nickname.

### Performer-mounted shot

Attach a camera facing back toward the performer. Snorricam is a named body-mount system. Specify a camera mounted to the performer and facing back at them; operator-worn stabilization is different.

Example direction: Mount the camera facing the performer so their torso stays fixed in frame.

### Handheld shot

Support the camera with the operator’s body or hands. 

Example direction: Shoot handheld with controlled movement; avoid unnecessary shake.

### Stabilized tracking shot

Support a travelling camera with a stabilising system. Steadicam and a motorized gimbal are different stabilization systems, not interchangeable names for the shot.

Example direction: Track through the doorway with a stabilizer; specify the route and final frame.

### Aerial shot

Place the camera on an airborne platform. 

Example direction: Shoot from an airborne platform, then specify the path and camera aim.

### Locked-off shot

Also used: Locked off, Static shot.

Keep camera position, orientation and framing fixed. 

Example direction: Lock off the camera and let the subject cross the frame.

### Extreme wide shot

Also used: EWS.

Show the setting with the subject very small in the frame. 

Example direction: Frame an EWS with the person small in the landscape.

### Wide shot

Also used: WS, Long shot, LS.

Show the subject together with a substantial part of the surroundings. Wide shot describes scale. Establishing shot describes the shot’s role in the sequence.

Example direction: Frame a WS that shows the person and the surrounding room.

### Full shot

Show the entire person from head to toe. Say head to toe if the full figure must remain visible. Do not rely on an unexplained abbreviation.

Example direction: Keep the person visible from head to toe.

### Medium wide shot

Also used: MWS, Medium long shot, MLS.

Frame a person roughly from the knees up; agree the precise crop. Cowboy shot or American shot (plan américain) is a related Western-derived framing. Agree the crop; labels alone do not fix the exact frame edge.

Example direction: Frame a medium wide, roughly knees up; agree the exact lower frame edge.

### Medium shot

Also used: MS.

Frame a person roughly from the waist up. Shot-size boundaries vary. State the intended crop as well as MS.

Example direction: Frame an MS, roughly waist up, with room for the hand gesture.

### Medium close-up

Also used: MCU.

Frame a person roughly from the chest up. MCU is useful shorthand; state the intended crop when precision matters.

Example direction: Frame an MCU, roughly chest up, with the eyes clearly readable.

### Close-up

Also used: CU.

Frame the face or object prominently so its details are easy to read. CU can describe a face or an object. It does not specify a focal length.

Example direction: Frame a CU on the face and agree the headroom before rolling.

### Extreme close-up

Also used: ECU, XCU.

Isolate a small detail, such as the eyes or part of an object. ECU and XCU are alternative abbreviations. Name the detail that must fill the frame.

Example direction: Frame an ECU on the eyes, excluding most of the face.

### Two-shot

Frame two subjects together. Two-shot means two people framed together. A three-shot is a different setup with three.

Example direction: Hold both people in a two-shot as they turn toward each other.

### Foreground frame

Place an object between the camera and part of the view. 

Example direction: Include a soft foreground edge to partially obscure the doorway.

Descriptive instruction, not a standardized nickname.

### Symmetrical composition

Balance the frame around an axis. 

Example direction: Center the doorway and balance the frame around its vertical axis.

Descriptive instruction, not a standardized nickname.

### Leading lines

Arrange visible edges to guide the eye. 

Example direction: Use the corridor edges to guide attention toward the person.

Descriptive instruction, not a standardized nickname.

### Establishing shot

Show the location and spatial relationships. 

Example direction: Show the building and its entrance to orient the next scene.

### Master shot

Also used: Master.

Cover the action and relationships in a sustained view. 

Example direction: Cover the whole exchange in a master before shooting the closer coverage.

### Insert shot

Also used: Insert.

Show a relevant detail within the scene. An insert emphasizes a detail within the scene. A cutaway shifts attention away from the main action; do not use the names interchangeably.

Example direction: Shoot an insert of the hand turning the key, matching the action in the wider shot.

### Reaction shot

Show a response to an event or another character. 

Example direction: Hold on the listener's response after the line.

### Continuous take

Also used: Oner, One-shot.

Present a sequence without a visible cut. Oner is crew shorthand. Distinguish an actual uninterrupted take from a sequence assembled to appear continuous.

Example direction: Play the scene as a oner; specify whether hidden joins are permitted.

### Arc at low camera height

Arc around the subject from a low camera height. Low camera height alone does not establish a low angle. State the upward aim separately.

Example direction: Arc around the subject at low camera height; specify whether the lens looks level or upward.

Descriptive instruction, not a standardized nickname.

### Overhead roll

Rotate the framing while the camera looks straight down. Roll turns the image around the viewing axis. An overhead orbit moves the camera around a point.

Example direction: Keep the camera directly overhead and roll the frame clockwise.

Descriptive instruction, not a standardized nickname.

### Aerial pull-back

Travel away from the subject from an airborne platform. 

Example direction: Pull back from the building in the air, keeping altitude constant.

Descriptive instruction, not a standardized nickname.

### FPV flight

Fly a planned route using a first-person control view. FPV means first-person view: the control method, not a compulsory route or speed.

Example direction: Plan the flight route and bank through the turn; FPV alone does not define the shot.

### Push past and reveal

Travel past a foreground subject to reveal what lies behind. 

Example direction: Push past the foreground figure to reveal the doorway beyond.

Descriptive instruction, not a standardized nickname.

### Arc against a subject turn

Move the camera around a subject turning in the opposite direction. 

Example direction: Arc right around the subject while they turn in the opposite direction.

Descriptive instruction, not a standardized nickname.

### Overhead descent

Descend from above toward the subject while maintaining a downward view. 

Example direction: Descend vertically with the lens pointing down, stopping clear of the subject.

Descriptive instruction, not a standardized nickname.

## Terminology references

- [Fitchburg State: The Shot List](https://openbooks.fitchburgstate.edu/preproduction/chapter/the-shot-list/): Shot-size abbreviations EWS, WS, MWS, MS, MCU, CU, ECU and OS; shot-list elements.
- [Yale Film Analysis: Cinematography](https://filmanalysis.yale.edu/cinematography/): Pan, tilt, framing scales, rack focus and the medium-long / American-shot terminology.
- [Shaner and Jones: Shots and Shot Plans](https://ptgmedia.pearsoncmg.com/images/chap05_0321238338/elementLinks/chap05_0321238338.pdf): Published textbook chapter: singles, dirty singles, inserts, masters and shot scale; pp. 116–127.
- [Columbia: Tracking (Trucking) Shot](https://filmglossary.ccnmtl.columbia.edu/term/tracking-trucking-shot/): Tracking is broader than a lateral move or following from behind.
- [Columbia: Camera Angle](https://filmglossary.ccnmtl.columbia.edu/term/camera-angle/): Eye-level, high-angle and low-angle terminology.
- [Columbia: Canted Angle (Dutch Angle)](https://filmglossary.ccnmtl.columbia.edu/term/canted-angle-dutch-angle/): Canted angle and Dutch angle are alternative names.
- [City College of San Francisco: Camera Movements](https://human.libretexts.org/Courses/City_College_of_San_Francisco/Video_Production_Handbook_I/08:_Video_Aesthetics/8.03:_Camera_Movements): Basic movement vocabulary, including push in / pull out and lateral trucking.
- [In Depth Cine: Crash Zoom](https://www.indepthcine.com/videos/crash-zoom): Practitioner explanation of rapid optical zooming in either direction.
- [Adobe: Cowboy Shot](https://www.adobe.com/au/creativecloud/video/production/cinematography/camera-shots-and-angles/cowboy-shot.html): Cinematographer commentary and the cowboy / American-shot names.
- [American Cinematographer: One Shot With Ed Sheeran](https://theasc.com/article/one-shot-ed-sheeran-cinematography/): Cinematographer Nyk Allen uses oner and distinguishes a true continuous take from hidden joins.
- [Snorricam: SC Pro Standard](https://www.snorricam.com/Products/SC-Pro-Standard): Manufacturer documentation for the named performer-mounted camera system.
- [Tiffen: Steadicam Volt 3](https://tiffen.com/pages/steadicam-volt-3): Steadicam is a named stabilization system; models can include electronic assistance.
