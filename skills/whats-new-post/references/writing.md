# Writing it

Voice and the specific moves that keep a roundup being read past the third entry.

## Voice

Plain, specific, unexcited. The interest comes from the facts, so the prose does not have to
supply any.

- **No em dashes, no double hyphens, no emoji, no exclamation marks.** Use a comma, a full
  stop, or restructure.
- Banned outright, because they describe nothing: unlock, seamless, leverage, dive into,
  supercharge, elevate, game changer, revolutionise, in today's fast paced.
- **Sentence case headings.** Not Title Case.
- Numbers as digits. Dates absolute, never "recently" or "last month".
- No unearned superlatives. Not "the best", not "the most powerful". **A claim from the data is
  more persuasive anyway**: "the only one in this window offering a hosted endpoint with no
  credentials" beats any adjective.
- Say what is not known plainly. "The publisher has not disclosed this" is a sentence. "No data
  available" is a form field.

## Length, and why uniform length reads badly

**Vary entry length deliberately.** The two or three genuinely interesting items get 200 words.
The competent ones get 80. When every entry is the same length the piece reads as a template
somebody filled in, and a reader who spots that stops reading.

The corollary: if you cannot find 200 words about your lead item, it is the wrong lead item.

## Keeping a reader to the end

A roundup is a page people abandon at entry three. What holds them, in order of effect:

**Open on the shape of the period, not on the first item.** The lede is the only place you get
to say something about the whole set. Spending it on an introduction to the article wastes the
one paragraph most people read.

**Front load the interesting ones.** Do not save the best for last in a piece nobody finishes.
Sort so entry one is the one you would tell a colleague about.

**Include one genuinely odd item.** Every window has something strange in it. A niche public
data set, a joke, one person's weekend project that does something nobody asked for. It is
usually the entry readers remember and quote, and it costs 80 words.

**Break the rhythm once.** After several entries of the same shape, one theme paragraph or one
short aside resets attention.

**End forward looking, and true.** What is still missing from the set, what the next window will
probably bring, what a reader can do now. Not a summary of what they just read.

## What ruins it

**A composed rationale.** The single most damaging thing you can write. If the summary field
says "MCP server for Acme", that is the whole of what you know. Writing a plausible paragraph
about why Acme built it is indistinguishable, to the reader, from the parts that are true, and
it contaminates the whole piece.

**Padding a thin window.** If the batch is dull, a short honest post is better than a long
inflated one. "Six arrived and none of them break new ground, but two are worth a look if you
work with X" is a perfectly good article.

**Treating a standing score as merit.** If you rank by one, say once what it measures. A score
for documentation completeness is not a quality ranking, and implying otherwise is a
misrepresentation a reader has no way to detect.

**Asserting a maker's self description.** Anything from the claims role is what the maker says
about itself. "Their security page claims SOC 2" and a link. Never "SOC 2 certified".

**Implying you tested anything.** Unless a field records a test that was run, the piece
describes what publishers published. Say so once, plainly, and the whole article becomes more
trustworthy rather than less.
